THREE SEAT is a first-person co-op game for one to four players. A film crew drives an old red farm truck through a zombie outbreak while their B-movie is being shot. Wreck the truck and the director yells CUT!, then Take 2 from the last mark. Leave his route and a slate reads WRONG SET! At the end a jury of four rates the film, and a good film earns a sequel: six films, each on its own route through the same county.

Deadlock is my team of one. I decided what the game is and how it looks. AI agents wrote the C++, the data, the tools and the scripts that generate every asset. They reached the engine through the Unreal Engine MCP when a job needed the live editor and, for nearly all of the work, through headless runs of the editor.

Most of the work was done by Claude Opus 5.5 and Claude Fable 5.2: Fable for coordination, art and design decisions, Opus for building, merging and tests. Technical work such as the online layer went to Codex (GPT-6.1 Sol and GPT-6 Astra).

This log covers the contest period, from the first commit on the afternoon of 12 November to the early hours of the 15th. Every number and picture comes from records written at the time. What I said was typed fast; it is given here by meaning.

Scene3Take1Datethe method

Made by code

Nobody modelled, sculpted, painted, animated or recorded anything by hand. Every model, painted surface, animation clip and sound comes out of a script that an AI agent wrote.

  1. 1 · Script

    An agent writes a generator

    three.js for the world, the chase car and six zombies; Blender Python for the truck, the weapons, the crew and four zombies. Every random choice is seeded.

    SourceArt/World/generator/gen.mjs
  2. 2 · Model

    The script writes the files

    Meshes, skeletons and clips. Animation is code too: each clip is a function of time.

    SourceArt/Zombies/generators
  3. 3 · Bridge

    Our own bridge into Unreal

    Headless editor scripts turn the files into engine assets and a World Partition level.

    SourceArt/World/unreal/world_import.py
  4. 4 · Painter

    Our own painter, in C++

    1,520 lines that repaint a model with strokes that follow its form. Out comes an ordinary texture.

    Tools/Painter/src/painter.cpp
  5. 5 · In bulk

    The whole world in two minutes

    Every atlas painted side by side on a rented cloud machine.

    SourceArt/World/paint/paint_batch.py
One barn through the chaingenerator, bridge, painter, game

The painter

The painter is a C++ program the agents wrote, with no third-party code. It reads a model's triangles, its unwrapped surface and its flat colours, and lays brush strokes along the form. Atlas size follows how close the player gets: 4096 pixels for a stop, 2048 for a building, 1024 for trees and props.

On the evening of the 13th I compared nine ways of putting paint on one shop and chose this one. The whole world was then painted on a rented cloud machine (a Colab G4 instance, on its processor cores; the painter has no GPU path): 30 atlases in 97 to 120 seconds, 6 to 8 minutes door to door. The laptop had needed over 12 minutes for one family.

What the scripts are

The generators and tools are 312 script files, about 78,000 lines, in the repository under SourceArt and Tools.

  • The world. One command writes a stretch of route as terrain tiles, road ribbons, props and placement: 1,618 terrain, 177 road and 483 prop meshes in the game.
  • The truck. One headless Blender script: body, cabin, parts, paint and the vehicle's data.
  • The zombies. Ten bodies sculpted in code from signed distance fields, each with 78 clips: 780 animation sequences, none animated by hand.
  • The chase car, the weapons, the crew. The car is one three.js file of 706 lines. The crew's faces and the jury's heads are drawn by Python.
  • The sounds. 184 of them, synthesized from sine waves and noise by four Python files of recipes. Nothing recorded, nothing downloaded. The game has no music.

Working without a window has one hard limit: a run without a renderer cannot take a picture. Check frames came from offscreen rendered runs, rationed on this laptop to one at a time.

Scene5Take1Date12 Nov

Day one: a blank project to a first drive

At 15:51 there was an empty project. By midnight there was a truck to climb into, a road, zombies to hit, a menu, a CUT! and a Take 2.

The first commit is a blank Unreal Engine 5.8.4 C++ project. At 16:24 the renderer is trimmed for the laptop's 4 GB card: no hardware ray tracing, no Lumen, no virtual shadow maps. That held for the whole jam.

The afternoon goes to the moving parts: the truck, the zombies and the hit system are compiled against 5.8 and wired into one level. By 17:19 a truck pawn stands in it.

From 18:00 the work fans out, one agent and one branch each for the player, combat, the game flow, sound and the world. 31 branches merge before midnight. The plan has one rule above the others, simple first, and one hold that I placed: no networking until a single-player game exists.

At 19:03 a headless script builds the first stretch of the world: 210 terrain tiles, 14 road chunks, 34 props. By 23:30 the whole first route is on main as one World Partition level, with a navigation mesh for the zombies.

Scene7Take1Date12 to 15 Nov

The cab and the mirrors

Most of the game is seen from one seat. On day one it was a windscreen and four gauges. By the third night it had hands on the wheel, three working mirrors and a line of key prompts.

The driver's seat12 to 15 Nov

The steering job hit a fault that looked like its own: when the driver looked sideways the whole cab vanished. Another agent had noticed that the truck's culling box was a flat slab at the chassis, taken from its one collision box. One message between the two saved the search. Three gameplay calculations read the same bounds, so they were pinned to the physics box before the merge.

A real flat mirror shows very little: from the driver's seat a door mirror covers about 8 by 15 degrees. The capture cameras were placed closer behind the glass so each mirror shows about twice that. Each picture is rendered at the size it has on screen, and only one mirror is captured per frame, in turn. The three cost 2.19 ms of GPU time at the higher quality, which I chose after seeing both in the driver's view.

The mirror frames found something nobody had looked for: at night the chase car's headlamps whited out the driver's whole view. The agent tuning the chase fixed it at the lamps.

Scene8Take1Date12 to 15 Nov

Zombies on the road

Running them over is the joy of the game, so most of the tuning went into how many there are, where they come from and what a hit does.

Zombies on the road12 to 14 Nov

More of them, from the right places

On the 14th zombies began to come out of the village doors. The cap on live zombies went from 25 to 36, and the number hit in a solo bot run from 20 to 49. Then the two-player bot failed four takes in a row at the fuel stop, the gunner bitten down while boarding, so the cap came back to 32 and the run passed. That evening I played and said the opposite:

There are far too few zombies.
My note 22, by meaning

My game log agreed: 31 truck hits in 26 minutes, nearly all in the first kilometre. The answer was a cast and a density per region, 13 regions in all, with a breather after each peak. A simulator drove the old and the new data at 60 km/h: 1.52 zombies in the lane per 100 m became 3.01, and the first contact came after 8.4 s instead of 15.7.

What a hit does

A hit has a set of outcomes: a bump, a burst, a body under the wheels, one over the roof, one through the windscreen. Weights decide which one plays. To retune them an agent took 365 real hits from twelve bot runs and replayed them through a copy of the chooser, 2,800 random seeds per candidate set. I picked from the chart: more bursting and flattening as the base, the rare outcomes as events, and ordinary hits that cost the truck nothing.

The flare

I asked for a trailer scene in which a flare is fired and the zombies run to it. The coordinator's note that evening reads: not built into the game, the flare is no lure yet. Three hours later it was one: a burning flare on the ground is a source in the noise system the zombies already listen to, and pulls those within 45 m.

The loading card14 Nov

A jury that disagrees

Four jurors watch every film, and each wants something different, so the same event splits them. The Director wants his plan obeyed. The Studio wants it cheap and loud. Granny Mae wants everyone home safe. The Critic is bored by competence and delighted by accidents.

I asked for the Critic to praise the truly absurd things at the wrap. The design that came back has a limit built in: he praises the single oddest fact of the film ("Eleven seconds with nobody at the wheel. The truck's finest work."), is bored by a clean one ("Competent. I checked my watch."), and turns on a crew that farms him. His words never change the rating.

The end of a film is a call sheet. It shows only rows with something to say, counts them up one by one, writes the rating by hand and slams a stamp: SEQUEL APPROVED or SEQUEL DENIED.

Scene10Take1Date13 to 15 Nov

A chase car tuned by trials

DEAD HEAT is a hot rod driven by a zombie. It sleeps beside the fuel stop, and once awake it chases the truck and tries to ram it. For a day it never touched it.

In the first full bot runs the car never came closer than 20.9 m. One run says little about a behaviour that depends on bends, speed and luck, so I asked for it to be tuned by many trials. An agent built a trial runner that teleports the truck to the fuel stop, starts the chase and logs how it ends: about 50 trials an hour.

The baseline showed where to look: ram attempts began about 150 m behind the truck and 53 of 70 timed out, while the seven that began within about 26 m all landed. The coordinator's direction was three plain rules: follow the truck's own trail, catch up by distance, ram only from inside 30 m.

The first tuned batch was worse, and the log of one trial showed why: the car joined the nearest point of the trail, which was its own parking spot, and drove the wrong way at 92 km/h. Two fixes later the car still ended most chases stuck, wedged on a verge or stopped dead on something the truck's larger wheels roll over. A second agent took over from the first one's notes and gave the chase a designed end, after three hits or 90 seconds.

Two things are written beside the last batch. A car stuck 70 m or more behind is put back on the trail out of sight, on average 1.9 times a chase. And all of it was tuned against the old truck: I asked for a faster truck the same evening. With the faster truck in the tree the chase tests ran 11 of 11. A retune against that truck was not done.

Scene11Take1Date12 to 15 Nov

Light, paint and dust

The look had to hold on a 4 GB laptop card, so each step was tried as a switch, shot before and after, measured, and then put to my eye.

On the evening of the 14th I asked what would raise the game's light further. A design agent tried eleven candidates as switches, each on the same four cameras. Five were dropped, most because nothing changed at 1080p: softer shadows, a newer ambient occlusion, screen-space global illumination, fog glow, lens flare. The ones kept change the mood and cost nothing that could be measured: a blue-violet sky light with a warm bounce from the ground, a film grade per time of day, volumetric shafts at sunset.

I then chose strengths from strips: the sunset set at two thirds, the day as it was, wind in the wheat strong.

Things that move

The last look job added what a moving truck should leave behind: dust from the rear wheels, snow spray at night, exhaust puffs, a splash in the ford. Its first measurement was bad, 9 ms a frame, and the cause was not the particles: a pool of instances rebuilt its render state every frame. With that fixed the cost is 0.2 ms.

Who decides what

I decided scope and art, by playing and by eye. A coordinator directed the agents. One agent kept the main branch. Every other agent had one job, one brief and one notes file.

Me

one person · the director

I decide what the game is and how it looks, and I play the packages. My decisions are recorded with the date and my words.

The coordinator

one AI session

Assigns jobs, reads the agents' diffs and frames while they work, tells them plainly what to change, and reports to me.

The keeper of main

one agent at a time · ten over the jam

The only one who touches main: tags, merges, builds the merge, runs the named tests, pushes, logs one line per step.

The agents

one job each

A brief says what to build and what not to. A notes file, current at every commit, lets a fresh agent take over.

The split between the models became a rule: Fable decides, Opus builds. A Fable job was planned as: decide, prove it with one or two results, then write the rest as a list another agent can follow without judgment calls.

What a brief looked like

The briefs that worked had the same eight parts in the same order: the goal in my own words; what is wrong now, as seen in the frames or the numbers; the simple version to build and what not to build; decisions already made, marked "don't reopen"; the order of steps, with the point where the agent must stop and show its work; the proof wanted; the report; the limits.

Scene13Take1Date14 Nov

One laptop

Everything ran on one machine: 32 GB of memory, twelve processor threads, a 4 GB graphics card, one installed copy of the engine. The machine set the pace more than the agents did.

Twelve editors, no memory left

At 16:00 on the 14th, twelve Unreal processes were up at once: tests, bot runs, world builds, frame captures. Committed memory stood at 48.3 of 48.8 GB. The fix was a gate: before any launch of the editor or the game, an agent runs a small script and starts only when it prints GO. The gate allowed six processes, then four, then three.

The step from six to four came from the build tool's own log. It asks for 1.5 GB of free memory per compile action, and with six editors open only two actions ran at a time on a machine that could run eight.

A lock that was a lottery

All clones of the project build through one machine-wide lock. The engine's build script keeps its lock file in the temp folder and every waiting build retries for it once a second, so later builds overtook earlier ones: one waited 24 minutes. A private temp folder per build made it wait in the build tool's own queue instead, which is in order.

Over six hours, 52 builds added up to 159 minutes of actual work, and agents had waited 20 to 45 minutes for each. The third cause was the same change being compiled once per clone. That ended on the last night, with one shared tree.

The usage limit, three times

At 13:58 on the 14th every agent stopped in the same minute: the account's five-hour usage window was spent. It happened again at 18:35 and at 23:19. Each agent had written its notes file minutes before, and each resumed with one message.

My rule was never to stop agents because a limit is near. What changed was the preparation: before the second limit the coordinator queued the work that needs no model as one detached script. Both bots reached the Gate while every agent was stopped, and the queue went on to package a release candidate.

The laptop itself took one more turn: at 08:18 on the 14th it rebooted for a system update and killed the agent that kept main. A fresh agent took over from the notes file.

Seven packages

The first package was broken by packaging, not by design: the interface's fonts and textures are loaded from code, and folders that only code refers to are left out of a package unless they are listed.

The build itself12 to 15 Nov

A fresh package compiles its graphics pipeline states on first launch, about 3,900 to 4,300 of them on this card. After the fix a first launch reaches the menu in 23.7 s instead of 72 s, behind a warm-up card that any key skips, and quitting takes about 4 s instead of 24 to 126.

What the log said

For each note the coordinator looked for evidence in the 240 KB log of my session. Four findings changed the work.

The truck that would not move. The log held 528 lines of contact with one object at under 1 km/h: the truck had climbed the roadside fence rails and hung there with its wheels off the ground.

The key that wrecked it. I wrote that R throws the truck up and does nothing. R was a developer's reset left on a player's key: it lifts the truck 2.5 m and drops it, and each drop was billed as a crash. In 22 seconds I pressed it seven times, the condition went from 98 to 0, and the game called CUT! Wrecked.

The fork. At one fork I went wrong in five passes out of six. A crew figure with an arrow card stood in the middle of the correct road, on purpose: the code comment said running one over is the point. A player steers away from a person in the road.

The smoke. Smoke is only a warning, and a test proved it: 0 to 30 km/h takes 2.67 s at condition 5 and 3.02 s at 100. But the game had shown smoke beside a dead truck and said nothing, so my reading was fair.

One tree, one train

Merge the clones, there are far too many builds. Three clones are enough for everything. Unreal tests last, or rarely.
My words, 14 Nov 22:34, by meaning

From 22:38 new agents no longer got a clone. An agent commits only its own paths, only after a green train that covers its last save.

  • It starved. For 11 minutes six agents never all paused for 60 seconds, so nothing was built. New rule: build at the latest 180 seconds after the first unbuilt change.
  • Train 9 was green and wrong. A header was saved while it compiled, so the binary held two versions of one struct, and two rendered runs crashed at frame 0. The train now notices that the tree changed during a build and builds again at once.

What the merge caught

  • The shared tree's first merge went red in two groups, Flow 12 of 13 and Truckplay 19 of 21: one map held the truck for 20 seconds at its start. It was fixed and merged 35 minutes later.
  • A test "hung" for ten minutes, and again through a 15 minute retry. Its command had reached Unreal unquoted.
  • One explanation I had been given, that a full stop against a wall stalls the engine, was wrong. A test corrected it.

What changed by morning

  • R works only while WHEELS UP! or STUCK shows. A rescue costs 10 condition and can never wreck the truck.
  • A crash sequence gets one bill, not five, and the engine restarts on throttle.
  • Two mechanics I approved on the spot: a kill-streak combo for the crew, and a pack of runners behind the truck on long straights for the gunner in the bed.

Scene17Take1Date14 to 15 Nov

The cut

The hardest note of the playtest was not a bug.

The game is monotonous: long, empty, straight roads, twice what they should be, and almost nothing in the first four or five minutes.
My note 26, by meaning

The complaint was measured before it was answered. The six films were 8.7 to 12.3 km long. Film 1, the longest, had 3.5 km of straights over 300 m, one of them 1.86 km. In 26 minutes I had reached 4.8 km: 39 per cent of the first film.

Shortening the generated world is hard: regions, stops, markers, navigation, the bot and the tests are all keyed to metres of road. The films driving less of it is not. A read-only agent wrote the plan with three maps. Cut A keeps the last 5 km of every film, where the set pieces are, and loses the opening and 10 of the 28 set pieces. Cut B, the planner's pick, keeps film 1 on the main road from the barn, 6.8 km with never more than 850 m between set pieces, and loses four.

Cut B was built as data that can be swapped back with one command, and the choice between B, A and no cut was put to me as one question with all three pictures. As this log closes, the films in the game are still the full ones. The cut is the one decision I have not made.

Proof

With nobody free to play every build, the game was driven by a bot, the roads by a sweep, and the rest by tests. The useful results were the failures.

The sweep drives every road at 30, 50 and 70 km/h and reports where the truck sticks. The bot plays a whole film, alone or as two players over the network, and writes down its takes and its frame times.

The last run was on the main that was pushed: film 1, solo, by day. The bot reached the Gate, 12,269 of 12,275 m, in 17 min 35 s on the run clock, with no crash, no softlock and no Take 2. Three stunts clean, top speed 79 km/h, lowest truck condition 82. It also found one bug, a false STUCK prompt each time a giant let go of the truck, which was fixed afterwards.

The route sweep's first pass had failures in 9 of 29 runs, all of them props standing in the road. A rerun after the fixes was clean. The online co-op harness passed 24 of 24 stages when it merged and 99 of 99 a day later.

What the bot found

  • A crash on Take 2. After a pile of sleeping zombies burst, restoring the take crashed: the pile's meshes were held in a plain container the garbage collector does not see.
  • A perfect jump into a wall. 22 of 22 chasm jumps landed clean, then stopped dead on the landing mound and rolled back into the gully. The mound was upside down.
  • The chasm in co-op. With a client driving, the game logged every jump as "lip 0 km/h, 0.0 s in the air": the jump was never registered for a networked truck.
  • A death spiral. A checkpoint saved the truck's low condition, so each restored take was wrecked again sooner.

A test that depended on the frame step

Late on the 14th a zombie test went red on main: feet sliding 0.78 against a limit of 0.75, in every clone and with old data. The agent was told to find what the measure depends on before touching the limit. It was the frame step: about 0.50 to 0.55 at a free 200 frames a second, 0.78 at the test runner's fixed 60. The test now counts only frames in which the same foot is down at both ends, and the limit stayed where it was.

Why Unreal

My conclusion from these four days: today Unreal Engine is the most solid engine for building a game with AI agents. These are the reasons this project showed.

  • It is text all the way down. The game's C++, its data and its configuration are plain files, and the editor itself runs Python. An agent can read and change all of it.
  • It runs without a window. Builds, commandlets, scripts, tests and whole game runs start from a command line, so many agents can work at once without sharing one editor.
  • Proof is built in. The automation framework gave every fix a named test, and a bot could play a whole film on a machine with no screen.
  • Packaging is one command. A script could do it with no agent running, and once did.
  • The MCP covers the rest. When a job needed the live editor, the Unreal Engine MCP gave an agent hands in it.

This is one project on one laptop, not a comparison of engines. The engine's one build lock and its appetite for memory cost us hours, and the conclusion stands.
